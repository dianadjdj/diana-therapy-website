## Coaching vs Therapy for Founders: How to Tell Which One You Need

Coaching works on the decisions in front of you. Therapy works on the inner life that shapes how you make them. For founders the two blur more than either profession likes to admit. Here is how to tell which one the thing in your way actually calls for.

I work as both an executive coach and a therapist. That is not the usual arrangement. Most people in my field pick one side and defend the border. So when a founder asks me whether they need coaching or therapy, they are usually surprised that I do not answer quickly. The honest answer depends on what is actually in their way, and the two are easier to confuse than the professions let on.

This guide is the answer I give. What each one is for, where they overlap, and how to tell, from the inside, which one the thing you are carrying actually calls for.

## The short version

Coaching works forward. It starts from where you are and moves toward something you want: a decision, a skill, a level of performance, a way of leading you have not grown into yet. It is largely present and future tense. A good coach is a thinking partner who holds you to your own aims and does not let you drift.

Therapy works deeper, and it works with time. It is a licensed clinical practice that engages your inner life, including the history and the patterns that shape how you show up now. Where coaching mostly assumes a steady foundation and builds on it, therapy can go to the foundation itself. It can also assess and treat things coaching cannot touch: depression, anxiety disorders, trauma, the clinical weight that sometimes sits under a founder's exhaustion.

The clearest structural difference is regulation. Therapy requires a graduate degree, supervised clinical hours, and a state license, and it operates inside a legal and ethical framework with real accountability. Coaching requires none of that. Anyone can call themselves a coach tomorrow. The good ones are very good, and there is a real profession with real standards inside the noise, but the floor is on the ground.

## Coaching vs therapy: a quick comparison

| Point of comparison | Coaching | Therapy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary focus | Goals, decisions, performance, the doing of the role | Inner life, history, patterns, and mental health |
| Time orientation | Mostly present and future | Present, but willing to work with the past |
| Regulation | Largely unregulated, no license required | Graduate degree and state license required |
| Can diagnose or treat a mental health condition | No | Yes |
| Typical question | What do I do about this, and who do I need to become to do it well | Why does this keep happening, and what is it costing me |
| Insurance | Not a healthcare service, paid privately or by the company | A clinical service, sometimes covered, often private-pay |

## What coaching is genuinely good at

When a founder is fundamentally steady and the obstacle is in front of them rather than under them, coaching is often the better tool. Sharpening a decision they keep circling. Building a skill the next stage of the company demands, whether that is delegating, giving hard feedback, or running a board they have outgrown their comfort with. Preparing for a specific high-stakes moment. Growing into a version of themselves the company now needs.

Coaching moves faster than therapy on these because it is allowed to be directive about the doing. A coach can say, try it this way, let us prepare for that conversation, here is the pattern I notice in how you run meetings. The International Coaching Federation describes coaching as partnering with clients in a process that inspires them to maximize their potential [[1]](#ref-1). In practice, with founders, that partnership is most useful when the raw material is a real external challenge and the founder has the internal stability to meet it.

## What therapy reaches that coaching cannot

Therapy is the right tool when the thing in the way does not respond to strategy. When a founder has reorganized their whole company around a problem and the problem is still there, the problem is usually not operational. When the exhaustion does not lift on the vacation. When the same conflict shows up with every co-founder, every head of sales, every partner. When something is happening in the body, in the sleep, in the hypervigilance that does not switch off. When a pattern predates the company by twenty years and the company is just the current stage it is playing out on.

Therapy can also do what coaching is not licensed or trained to do: recognize and treat a clinical condition. This matters for founders specifically, because founders carry elevated risk. Michael Freeman's research at UC San Francisco, the most frequently cited work on the subject, found that 72% of entrepreneurs surveyed reported a lifetime history of at least one mental health concern, compared to 48% of a comparison group [[2]](#ref-2). A coach who runs into depression or a trauma response in a client has hit the edge of their scope. A therapist is where that work belongs. The American Psychological Association describes psychotherapy as treatment, delivered by trained and licensed professionals, for mental health conditions and the emotional difficulties that interfere with a person's life [[3]](#ref-3).

## Why the line is blurrier for founders than either profession admits

Here is where I part company with the standard framing. The two fields keep a clean, bright line between them, and a lot of that line is professional protectionism rather than a real feature of the work. In practice, with founders, coaching and therapy bleed into each other constantly, because a founder's inner life and their company are not two separate things.

The hire a founder cannot make is, on the surface, a coaching problem. Underneath, it is often grief for the executive they imagined this stage would give them. The strategic decision they keep deferring is sometimes a decision that has already been made in their body and is waiting for permission. The feedback they cannot deliver is tangled with how feedback was delivered to them at home, thirty years earlier. A skilled coach keeps bumping into the inner material. A skilled therapist keeps bumping into the operating reality. Pretending these are separate rooms with a wall between them serves the professions more than it serves the founder.

What I do not think is that the two should be collapsed into one relationship. The line is overdrawn conceptually, but there are good reasons to keep the roles distinct in practice, which is the next section. My point is narrower and, I think, true: a founder is one person, and the thing weighing on them rarely arrives pre-sorted into coachable and clinical. Part of the work of a first conversation is figuring out which register it actually lives in.

## Can the same person be both your coach and your therapist?

Not at the same time, for the same person, inside one relationship. This is not a technicality. Coaching and therapy run on different contracts, different boundaries, and different power dynamics, and holding both roles for one person at once creates what clinical ethics codes call a dual relationship, which is treated with real caution. The AAMFT Code of Ethics, which governs my license, is explicit that therapists take care around multiple relationships that could impair judgment or risk harm [[4]](#ref-4). The concern is not fussiness. It is that the two relationships can quietly undermine each other, and the client is the one who absorbs the cost.

What a provider trained in both can do is think in both registers, and be honest with you about which one you are in. When I am someone's therapist, I am their therapist, and the operating context is material we work with clinically, not a coaching engagement wearing a therapy coat. When the work a founder needs is genuinely coaching, I can say that, and sometimes the most useful thing I do is help them find the right coach rather than keep them in the wrong chair. The skill is not doing both jobs at once. It is knowing the difference well enough to place the work where it belongs.

## How to tell which one you need

A few honest tests, from the inside.

**Does it move when you strategize?** If the obstacle responds to a better plan, a new skill, a sharper decision, it is probably coachable. If you have strategized around it for months and it has not moved, the problem is likely not living where your strategy can reach it.

**Is it in your body?** Sleep that will not come, a chest that stays tight, a vigilance that does not switch off on the weekend, a fuse that keeps getting shorter. The body is usually reporting something the strategy deck is not. That is therapy's territory.

**How old is it?** If the pattern in the way is older than the company, if you can find earlier versions of it in your twenties, in your family, in every prior job, coaching will keep meeting the current version while the root stays put. Therapy is built to work at that depth.

**What is the question underneath?** If the real question is what do I do, coaching. If the real question is why does this keep happening to me, therapy. Most founders, if they sit with it for a minute, already know which question they are actually asking.

And often the answer is both, in sequence or in parallel, held by two different people. There is no failure in that. A founder can have a coach sharpening the doing and a therapist working the inner life at the same time, and the two supports do different jobs. If you are still not sure whether what you are carrying is clinical, [Do I Actually Need Therapy?](../blog/do-i-need-therapy-executives.html) walks through the question in more depth, and [Business Therapist vs Regular Therapist](business-therapist-vs-regular-therapist.html) covers what to look for if therapy is the answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between coaching and therapy?

Coaching works forward from where you are now toward goals you set, mostly in the present and future: decisions, performance, and the doing of the job. Therapy is a licensed clinical treatment that works with your inner life and history, including the patterns and wounds that shape how you show up. Coaching is largely unregulated and requires no license. Therapy requires a graduate degree and a state license, and it can diagnose and treat mental health conditions. For founders the two often overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

### Do I need a coach or a therapist?

If the thing in your way is a decision, a skill, or a performance question, and you are otherwise steady, coaching is usually the right fit. If the thing in your way keeps returning no matter how you strategize around it, if it shows up in your body or your sleep, or if it predates the company, that is the territory of therapy. Many founders benefit from both, held in separate relationships.

### Can one person be both my coach and my therapist?

Not at the same time, in the same relationship. Holding both roles for the same person creates a dual relationship that clinical ethics codes treat with caution, because the contracts, boundaries, and power dynamics differ. A provider trained in both can move fluidly between coaching and clinical thinking, but they should be clear about which relationship you are in, and the two should not be blurred inside one engagement.

### Is executive coaching covered by insurance?

No. Coaching is not a healthcare service, so insurance does not cover it, and it is typically paid privately or by the company. Therapy can be covered by insurance because it is a licensed clinical service, though many therapists who work with founders operate on a private-pay basis to protect privacy and to sustain longer-form work.
